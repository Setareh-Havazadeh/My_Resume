It’s not this—it’s that

There’s a sentence shape every language model adores: state what a thing isn’t, then pivot—with a dash—to what it gloriously is. It’s not a tic; it’s a technique. Used well it sings. Used constantly it’s a tell. Placeholder copy for the Portfolio Starter template.

By Your Name January 2, 2026 3 min read

The shape

You know it the second you see it. “It’s not a bug—it’s a feature.” “This isn’t a redesign—it’s a rethink.” “We’re not selling software—we’re selling time.” The move is always the same: deny the small, obvious framing, then—beat—reveal the bigger, flattering one.

Deny the small thing. Hold the beat. Reveal the big one.

Why it works

It works because contrast is the cheapest drama in writing. By naming what something isn’t first, you give the reader a wrong answer to discard—and discarding it feels like insight. The em-dash is load-bearing here: it’s the hinge the whole sentence swings on. Replace it with a comma and the magic leaks out.

“It’s not a sentence—it’s a tiny magic trick.” — this template, still talking about itself

Why it grates

Because a trick repeated stops being a trick—it becomes a habit, and habits are how readers spot a machine. When every paragraph negates-then-elevates, the rhythm flattens into a drone. It’s not persuasion anymore; it’s wallpaper. (See what happened there? Now imagine forty of those in a row.)

Using it well

Ration it. One well-placed “not this—but that” per page lands like a punchline. Five lands like a tic. The goal isn’t to never use the shape—it’s to make each one feel earned. And that, conveniently, is the whole thesis of this template: keep the structure, spend the flourishes carefully, and let your actual ideas carry the weight.

Now—you guessed it—go replace every word of this with something that’s true about you.
